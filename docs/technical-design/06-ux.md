# UX Design

One principle drives every screen: each role opens the app with a different
question in mind, and the landing page must answer that question without a click.

| Role | Opens the app asking | Landing page answers it with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employee | "How much did I make, and how many vacation days do I have?" | Four stat cards + balance cards above the fold |
| Manager | "Is anyone waiting on me?" | Pending-approvals count as the first element |
| Bookkeeper | "What is unfinished?" | Task list: unassigned slips, unpublished periods, missing entitlements |

## 1. Employee

### Dashboard (`/employee`)

Above the fold: **Latest net pay** (with "vs. previous month" delta and the base
month named), **12-month average net**, **Deduction rate**, **Vacation days
available**.

Below: net pay trend line over 12 months (gross as a lighter second line), a
deduction donut by category, and the three most recent pay slips with download
buttons.

Empty state matters here — a new employee has no pay slips, and the dashboard must
not look broken. Instead of zeroed charts: "Your first pay slip will appear here
once your bookkeeper publishes it," with the leave balance section still live,
since entitlements exist from day one.

### Pay slips (`/employee/payslips`)

Year selector in the URL (`?year=2026`), grouped by year, showing month, gross,
net, deductions, and a download button. Download is a `<form>` posting to an
action: spinner while the signed URL is minted, then the browser download starts.
No new tab, no visible URL to copy.

The detail page (`/employee/payslips/[id]`) shows the full component breakdown as
two columns — earnings and deductions — with each line's 12-month sparkline, so
"why is this month different?" is answerable on the page instead of by opening two
PDFs side by side.

### Time off (`/employee/time-off`)

Balance cards per leave type showing entitled / used / pending / available, then
the request history newest first with status badges.

The request form is the most-used form in the app, so it does the arithmetic:
picking a date range shows **"7 calendar days = 5 working days"** and **"You will
have 9 days remaining"** before submitting. Weekends and company holidays are
visually marked in the picker, and the submit button is disabled with an inline
explanation when the balance is insufficient — never a surprise error after
clicking.

Pending requests show a Cancel button; decided ones show the decision, who made
it, when, and the note.

## 2. Manager

### Approvals (`/manager/approvals`)

The manager's primary job, so it is the first nav item and the dashboard's first
card. Each pending request is a card showing: employee name, leave type, dates,
working days, their remaining balance after this request, **who else on the team is
already away on those dates**, the reason, and Approve / Reject.

That overlap warning is the single most valuable element in the manager view.
Approving leave without knowing two other people are already out is the actual
failure mode of the spreadsheet process this product replaces.

Rejecting opens a dialog requiring a note. Approving is one click with an optimistic
badge flip and an Undo toast for a few seconds — Undo is UI affordance only; once
committed, reversal means a new request, per the state machine.

### Team (`/manager/team`)

Table of direct reports: name, role, department, days available, pending requests.
Read-only throughout — no edit affordances render at all, rather than buttons that
fail on click.

Per-employee pages show leave history, entitlements, and pay slip **metadata**
(month, gross, net). Whether the PDF is downloadable depends on
`companies.managers_can_view_payslip_files`; when disabled, the UI says "Pay slip
files are restricted by company policy" instead of hiding the row silently, so the
manager understands the boundary rather than assuming a bug.

### Calendar (`/manager/calendar`)

Month grid of who is away when, approved and pending distinguished by fill. This is
the planning view; the approvals queue is the action view.

## 3. Bookkeeper

### Dashboard (`/bookkeeper`)

A work queue, not a chart wall: "12 pay slips awaiting assignment", "March 2026 not
published", "3 employees missing 2026 entitlements" — each a link straight to the
screen that resolves it.

### Period workspace (`/bookkeeper/periods/[periodId]`)

The screen where the bookkeeper spends most of their time, built as a two-pane
layout:

- **Left:** drag-and-drop upload zone accepting multiple PDFs, with per-file
  progress, size/type rejection inline, and a duplicate warning from the checksum
  check.
- **Right:** unassigned files list. Each row shows the filename, a PDF preview
  thumbnail, and an employee combobox pre-filled with the filename-parsed
  suggestion marked "suggested — confirm".

A progress header reads "34 of 40 assigned", and **Publish** stays disabled until
that reaches parity, with the disabled reason stated next to it. Publishing opens a
confirmation dialog naming the consequence explicitly: "This will make 40 pay slips
visible to 40 employees. This cannot be undone."

Confirmation dialogs are reserved for exactly this kind of irreversible, wide-blast
action. Putting one on every delete trains users to click through them, so the one
that matters stops being read.

### Employee salary editing (`/bookkeeper/employees/[id]/salary`)

Period-by-period table with editable component rows. Totals are recalculated live
and shown next to the PDF's stated total, with a mismatch highlighted — the
bookkeeper is transcribing from a PDF, and catching the typo at entry time is worth
more than any later report.

Save is explicit, dirty state is visible, and navigating away with unsaved changes
warns. Each save writes an audit row; the last editor and timestamp are shown in
the header.

### Employees and documents

Employee list with trigram search, filters for status and department, and inline
"invite to portal" for employees without a login. Document upload targets an
employee (Form 106, Form 101, contract) or the whole company, with the year for
tax forms.

## 4. Cross-cutting

**Navigation** is role-scoped: an employee never sees a Manager nav item. Combined
with the layout guards and RLS, that means three layers agree on what a role can
reach, and the UI never shows a door that is locked.

**Loading** uses `loading.tsx` skeletons per segment that match the final layout's
dimensions, so nothing jumps as data arrives. Charts reserve their height before
data lands.

**Errors** appear where the failure happened: field-level for validation, a card in
place of a broken widget, a toast for action failures. Never a full-page error for a
localized problem.

**Empty states** always name the next step or the reason nothing is there. "No
pending requests" is fine; a blank panel is not.

**Mobile** matters for the employee view specifically — checking a pay slip on a
phone is the most common real-world use. Stat cards stack, tables become cards,
charts scroll horizontally. The bookkeeper workspace is desktop-first without
apology: it is a multi-file batch tool, and pretending otherwise would make it
worse on both.

**RTL** throughout, with numeric tables in LTR islands so amounts and dates align
the way accountants read them.

## 5. Trade-offs we are choosing to accept in v1

Worth stating plainly, since the assignment asks what we would improve later:

- **No in-app notifications or email.** A manager learns about a request by opening
  the app. Email is the obvious v2 addition and needs a queue to avoid coupling
  request submission to an external service's uptime.
- **Single-level approval.** No delegation when a manager is on vacation, which is a
  real gap for a company where the manager is also the person who takes August off.
- **No pay slip parsing.** The bookkeeper types the numbers. PDF extraction is
  attractive but wrong to guess at: a mis-parsed salary shown to an employee as fact
  is worse than a slower manual path.
- **No bulk operations for managers.** Approving 20 requests is 20 clicks. Reviewing
  each one individually is arguably correct for leave decisions, but it will be
  asked for.
